Integrating the Orano "Wire Style"

You're right—just throwing random 3D wireframes in the background looks like generic template slop. For it to feel like an engineering masterpiece, the wireframe must be mechanically tied to the content.

Here are three ways we can integrate that Orano-style wire aesthetic deeply into the storyline:

A

The Continuous Spline (The "Bridge")

How it works: A single, glowing wire (a spline) enters from the left of the screen. As you scroll horizontally, this wire acts as your guide. When you enter a department (e.g., Formula EV), the wire dynamically splits and weaves itself into a 3D wireframe schematic of a vehicle chassis. As you leave, it collapses back into a single thread.

Why it works: It perfectly visualizes your subtitle: "Bridging scientific research and production software." The wire is the bridge.

B

The Interactive Blueprint Nodes

How it works: Instead of traditional HTML cards, your project details live inside the wireframes. As you enter the AI Agent department, a complex wireframe neural graph draws itself. Your achievements (e.g., "Sub-100ms latency") are glowing nodes on this wireframe. Clicking a node expands the text.

Why it works: The 3D element isn't just decoration; it is the actual UI architecture. It forces the user to engage with the engineering directly.

C

The Engineer's HUD (Data Overlay)

How it works: The background is pitch black. The wireframes (vectors, physics waves, circuitry) are massive, taking up the whole screen, rendering with fine, precise WebGL lines. Your text content scrolls horizontally over it on frosted glassmorphic panels. The wireframes in the back react mathematically to your scroll position and cursor.

Why it works: It gives that hyper-premium, cinematic Orano feel. The typography stays readable on the glass, while the math/engineering happens behind it like a massive laboratory display.
